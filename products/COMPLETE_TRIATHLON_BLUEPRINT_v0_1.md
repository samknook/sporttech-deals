## Chapter 1 — One sport, three disciplines
The swim, bike and run are connected. The athlete who gains two minutes on the bike but loses ten minutes on the run has not improved the final outcome. This is why a triathlon plan should begin with **total elapsed time** rather than three independent personal records.

Your total modeled time is:
`Swim + T1 + Bike + T2 + Run`

Transitions count. Distance and pacing are not fixed across all organizers, so read the event guide before entering your values.

**Exercise — Your baseline.** Write down a swim pace per 100 m, a cycling speed estimate in km/h, a running pace per kilometer, and two transition estimates. Use values supported by relevant training rather than your best single-discipline performances. Enter them in the free [TriZentro Race Dashboard](https://samknook.github.io/sporttech-deals/race-dashboard.html). Save the result; then repeat with a deliberately conservative scenario.

**Reflection:** Which estimate is least certain? What test or training session could make that estimate more reliable?

## Chapter 2 — Choose the right distance and goal
Different competitions have different demands. Common Sprint and Olympic examples are 750 m / 20 km / 5 km and 1.5 km / 40 km / 10 km respectively. A typical branded 70.3 involves approximately 1.9 km / 90 km / 21.1 km; not every event called "middle distance" uses identical distances. World Triathlon also has other middle-distance formats. Use the **actual organizer distances** when creating a plan.

A useful goal has three layers:
- **Process:** follow your pacing and nutrition plan, check the official rules, and stay adaptable.
- **Outcome:** a realistic finish range, recognizing weather and course uncertainty.
- **Stretch:** a faster result that should never override safety or sound judgment.

**Exercise — three goals.** Write your race date, your main reason for racing, one outcome goal and three process goals. Example process goals: check the course briefing, rehearse transitions twice, and test your race-day nutrition before race week.

## Chapter 3 — Swim smarter
For many beginners the open-water environment is the biggest source of uncertainty. Swimming a measured distance in a pool is useful, but race-day sighting, other swimmers, temperature and waves add demands.

**Practical skill ladder:**
1. In safe, supervised conditions, build comfort with continuous easy swimming.
2. Practise breathing patterns that stay relaxed at a sustainable effort.
3. Add short sighting practice: look forward briefly and return to a streamlined position.
4. With appropriate local safety arrangements, rehearse open-water starts and turns.
5. Practise the exit from swimming into an easy walk or jog before a transition drill.

Never practise open-water swimming alone, and follow event wetsuit and water-safety rules.

**Exercise — two observations.** After an easy swim note: how even were your lengths, and how controlled was your breathing? Improvement in race readiness is not only a faster split.

## Chapter 4 — Bike smarter
Cycling often consumes the most race time, but the best standalone time trial effort is not necessarily the best triathlon bike effort. Wind, climbing, road surface, aerodynamics and nutrition can substantially change average speed.

Plan the bike leg around a sustainable effort that leaves you able to run. For athletes already using calibrated power data, power and perceived effort can help explain why speed alone fluctuates. Without a power meter, consistent perceived effort and appropriate training benchmarks remain useful. Do not prescribe a fixed fraction of FTP to all users as if it were universally safe or optimal.

**Exercise — brick test.** In a suitable training week, finish an ordinary bike session with a short comfortable run if your training history supports it. Note how the first ten minutes feel, whether the bike pace was sustainable, and what you would change. Increase load progressively and use recovery days.

**Decision checklist:** Is your setup comfortable and permitted by the race? Can you safely handle the bike? Do you know the no-drafting and equipment rules? Are the brakes, tyres and repair kit checked?

## Chapter 5 — Run smarter
Running after cycling is different from a fresh running race. The early kilometers can feel misleadingly easy or awkward. A controlled start is often more robust than chasing an aggressive first split.

**Three-part plan:**
- First section: settle into a controlled effort and assess how the legs respond.
- Middle section: maintain form, adjust to temperature and terrain, and follow tested fueling.
- Final section: increase effort only when it remains manageable and safe.

**Exercise — readiness notes.** Record your best *repeatable* running pace after a bike session, not just your standalone PB. Use the Race Dashboard to explore how a 10-second-per-kilometer difference changes your outcome.

## Chapter 6 — Transitions: the fourth discipline
Transitions need practice more than fancy equipment. Arriving at the rack disoriented or missing one item can waste time and increase stress.

**T1 walk-through:** know where your bike is; remove swim equipment as permitted; use the designated area; put on the helmet before touching or moving the bike; obey mounting lines and event instructions.

**T2 walk-through:** dismount at the required line, rack your bike as directed, remove the helmet only when allowed, switch to running gear, and exit on the correct route.

**Exercise — timed rehearsal.** With the race rules in mind, rehearse your planned sequence at an easy pace. Identify one unnecessary step to remove.

## Chapter 7 — Train for the life you live
A successful plan respects work, family, sleep, injury history and mental load. More training is not automatically better training.

Use a weekly structure that is **adaptable**:
- Choose which sessions matter most to your present abilities and target event.
- Include sessions for swim, bike and run as your experience and availability allow.
- Make room for recovery, lighter periods, and technique work.
- Increase volume or intensity cautiously rather than making sudden jumps.
- Reassess after illness, a missed week or persistent fatigue rather than "making up" everything.

**Weekly reflection sheet:**
- What did I complete?
- What felt harder than expected?
- Did I recover adequately?
- What is one adjustment for next week?

The expanded edition may include sample *illustrative* training weeks for different experience levels. They must be professionally reviewed before being sold as instructional plans.

## Chapter 8 — Nutrition and hydration are rehearsed, not guessed
A race-fueling plan is an experiment in real training conditions. Carbohydrate needs and tolerability vary with duration, intensity, gut training and individual physiology. Hydration depends on conditions, availability and personal sweat losses; **drinking excessively is not a safe strategy**.

Avoid universal mandatory intake targets or the assumption that every athlete should consume the same gels and bidons. Start with products and amounts you tolerate, practise on longer sessions, and refine in similar conditions. Your nutrition strategy needs to include what is actually available on course.

**Exercise — inventory.** Write each product you have tested, grams of carbohydrate per serving, how you carried it, what you actually consumed and how you felt. Use [TriZentro Race Fuel Planner](https://samknook.github.io/sporttech-deals/race-fuel-planner.html) to calculate a *self-selected* supply list. This is a math tool, not a prescription.

## Chapter 9 — Race week and race morning
Race week is not the moment to radically change equipment, workouts or food. Check registration, check-in and transition-area hours; inspect the forecast, route changes and safety briefings.

**Day-before checklist:**
- Confirm check-in, briefing and timing.
- Inspect the bike and required equipment.
- Pack tested clothing, nutrition and spare essentials.
- Prepare the route, transport and an alternate plan.
- Prioritize rest and predictable routines.

**Race-morning checklist:**
- Check official timings again.
- Allow time for transport, parking and moving between event areas.
- Check your assigned rack and follow helmet/race regulations.
- Review the course and your nutrition supplies.
- Arrive at the start area with a realistic buffer.

Use the [Race Day Timeline Planner](https://samknook.github.io/sporttech-deals/race-day-timeline.html) as a planning aid, never as a substitute for organizer instructions.

## Chapter 10 — Build your one-page race decision sheet
Create one race sheet that has:
1. Event name, date, wave, registration and emergency contacts kept privately.
2. Official swim/bike/run distances and cut-off times.
3. Conservative, baseline and ambitious timing scenarios.
4. Transition sequence and location-specific notes.
5. Tested food/drink inventory and likely pickup points.
6. Travel and check-in timeline.
7. A short contingency plan for heat, wind or delays.

**Worked 70.3 arithmetic example** (not an athlete forecast):
- Swim 35:00; T1 04:00; Bike 2:35:00; T2 03:00; Run 1:42:00.
- Total = 4:59:00.

This does not establish whether that combination is physically achievable for any particular athlete. Test assumptions against training and course conditions.
